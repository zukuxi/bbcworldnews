'I thought I would die': Survivors describe shock and horror of powerful Japan earthquake

- Published

Business was winding down for the day at Sendan Shokudo, a diner in Japan's south-western city of Yatsushiro, when the whole building suddenly lurched.

Everything on the kitchen shelves cascaded to the floor; bowls and glasses shattered.

The restaurant's co-owner, 75-year-old Hiroko Ogata, had just walked in.

"I was surprised, and scared," she told BBC News. "I went under one of the tables right away, but the table moved sharply towards the right, left, right, left, with me."

There's a reason Ogata knew how to react so swiftly: she has lived in Yatsushiro, in central Kumamoto prefecture, her whole life.

The area is located on an active tectonic fault system and is no stranger to tremors. In 2016, a pair of earthquakes killed 277 people and injured 2,800 others.

But Ogata has "never experienced this big of a jolt".

"The last quake 10 years ago was nothing like this one," she said. "I thought I would die."

At least 13 people have been killed in the wake of the 6.8-magnitude earthquake that hit Kumamoto late on Tuesday.

Of that number, three died in the Aeon shopping mall in Kumamoto city, where a number of people remain unaccounted for.

Rescue efforts are under way, but Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi has warned that emergency services are in a "race against time".

'Big jolts came one after another'

Just a few hundred metres away from the Aeon mall, Yui - who preferred not to use her real name for this report - was sitting in the back office of her cake shop when the quake hit.

"I couldn't tell which was the sound of the earthquake and which was the sound of the explosion," she told the BBC.

"All I could do was hold the chair I was sitting in as big jolts came one after another," she added, appearing to still be in shock.

"I didn't know what to feel; I couldn't even feel scared in such a situation. Even now I don't know what to feel, and I don't know how to explain."

Authorities and emergency responders mobilised quickly. Shortly after the tremor struck, Takaichi put 4,600 personnel on the ground to assist with recovery, and said that teams would work "tirelessly through the night" to assess and monitor the damage in affected areas.

Tens of thousands of people have experienced power and water outages across Kumamoto City and at least 45 medical facilities have been damaged.

The exterior walls of the 17th century Kumamoto Castle, in the city's centre, came toppling down in a plume of dust. The worship hall of the 19th Century Yatshushiro Shrine completely collapsed.

One local resident Michal Posan said there is "no water or food left in the stores".

Posan, 25, has been living in Kumamoto for two years, and was at home at the time of the quake.

"My TV fell on me, my mirror smashed and the roads around me were damaged," she told the BBC – adding that she had started preparing evacuation bags amid the "constant aftershocks".

Another resident said aftershocks were still rattling the city hours after the initial tremor.

"People are wary of another earthquake on the way," he said. "The 2016 earthquake [was] followed by a second days later."

Experts are also concerned. Shinji Kiyomoto of Japan's Meteorological Agency has asked the public to "remain vigilant" for further earthquakes. He too referenced 2016's deadly series of earthquakes, which struck days apart.

"We cannot rule out the possibility that an even larger quake could follow this time as well," he said. "We urge people to remain on alert."

Kyushu island, where Kumamoto prefecture is located, is a spiderweb of active fault zones. These include the Hinagu fault, which runs about 81km through the island's heartland.

That fault zone was partially activated in the 2016 quakes – but experts have long warned that its remaining, unruptured sections could generate a major earthquake.

Assessments by the government's earthquake research committee suggest that tremors with magnitudes as high as 7.5 could strike along the inland section of the zone. If the whole Hinagu fault were to rupture, the magnitude could reach 8.

In the meantime, locals like Ogata and Yui are having to pick up the pieces and try to restore some kind of normality – even as the shockwaves, both geological and psychological, continue to occur.

"The whole of my place is messy, and I don't know where to stand – but we have to earn our living by restarting our restaurant as soon as possible, so I am cleaning the place today," Ogata said on Wednesday.

"But when I try to work for a bit, an aftershock occurs and I have to stop – then I do some work and we have another aftershock… So it is going so slowly.

"If we keep having this many aftershocks it will be very stressful."

Yui's cake shop remains closed, as she assesses the extent of the damage.

"I haven't checked whether the machines such as the oven are working," she said. "After I check those, I will have an idea whether I can go back to business or not."
